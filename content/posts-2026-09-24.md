### English
```
We were asked again this week, so here is the honest answer: yes, every plant we sell is toxic to cats, dogs and children. 🐈

But not in the way most people fear, and the difference matters.

The whole aroid family stores calcium oxalate as microscopic needle-shaped crystals. It is not a systemic poison. There is no toxin travelling to the liver. Biting a leaf drives thousands of sharp crystals into the mouth, and the result is immediate and mechanical: burning, drooling, refusing food.

That mechanism is also the animal's protection. It hurts on the first bite, so almost nothing keeps chewing. The typical case is one experimental mouthful, a miserable hour, and a cat that never touches that plant again.

Do straight away: wipe the mouth out, offer something cold (milk or yoghurt, the calcium binds some of the oxalate).
Do not: induce vomiting. It drags the crystals across the same tissue a second time.
Vet if: swelling that affects breathing or swallowing, drooling past two hours, vomiting that will not stop.

And a high shelf is not a solution for a cat. A closed cabinet is, and the plants prefer the humidity anyway.

The one thing we will not do is tell you a plant is pet safe when it is not 👇
```

**Entry:** pinkleaf.co.il/encyclopedia/how-much-light-israeli-apartment.html

### English
```
"Bright indirect light" is not an instruction. It is a specific window, at a specific distance, facing a specific way. ☀️

And in Israel that matters more than usual, because the light here is strong. Not jungle strong, desert strong. Most care guides were written in northern Europe, which makes "put it in a sunny window" advice to burn the plant.

Orientation:
East is best for aroids. Gentle morning sun, then soft light.
North is safe all summer but can be too weak in winter.
West is the trap: afternoon sun in a hot room.
South only behind a sheer curtain.

Distance matters more than orientation, and it is where most people go wrong. One metre back from the window is about a quarter of the light. A room that feels bright to you is dim to a plant, because your eyes adapt and a light meter does not.

The shadow test, costing nothing: hold your hand 30 cm above the leaf.
Sharp dark shadow = too much, especially for variegation.
Soft shadow with a defined edge = correct.
No shadow at all = the plant is sitting in the dark.

And the expensive symptom: a new leaf coming in green on a variegated plant. That is not missing fertilizer, it is missing light. Variegation is energy the plant can only afford when it has enough to spare.
```

```
"Can I put the Monstera on the balcony?" Yes, some of them, part of the year. And what decides it is not the heat and not the winter. 🌬️

It is the sharav.

35°C in humid coastal air is a growing day. The same 35°C at 15 percent humidity with wind is a leaf losing water faster than roots can replace it. You come home to a plant that crisped in a single afternoon.

A sharav day is the one day a balcony plant has to come inside. The forecast gives you a day of warning. Use it.

Balcony aspect:
North is the good one. Bright, never direct.
East works until about eleven, then needs shade.
West is the trap: afternoon sun on concrete that keeps radiating after sunset.
A glassed-in balcony is a greenhouse, not outdoors. Ten degrees above the outside air.

What always stays inside: anything strongly variegated. White tissue has no chlorophyll and no protection, and it burns at light levels green tissue enjoys.

And winter? In most of the country it is the easy half. An Alocasia will go dormant and look dead, and that is normal. Keep it dry and it comes back in spring.
```

### English
```
Most of what sells as rare is not. Some of it is not even the plant on the label. 🔍

And it is almost never a dishonest seller. It is sellers repeating what they were told.

How to check, before you pay:

The kind of variegation. Hard-edged blocks that follow the leaf's structure mean chimeric, and that is the kind worth money. A fine even speckle of dots spread across the whole leaf suggests a virus. It will infect the rest of your collection. Walk away.

The newest leaf, not the biggest. If the old ones are dramatic and the new one is mostly green, the plant is reverting and you are buying the past.

The growing point. That is where the chimera lives. Damaged means it may never produce variegation again.

And the ratio everyone gets wrong: a plant that is 80 percent white is not the best one, it is the most fragile. No chlorophyll means no energy.

The one question that separates a good seller from a bad one: "where did this plant come from, tissue culture or a cutting?" Both answers are fine. An evasive answer is an answer.

And always ask for a photo of the actual plant, taken today, with the whole pot in frame.
```
